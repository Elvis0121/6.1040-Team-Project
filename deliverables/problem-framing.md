# Problem framing


## Domain
Our problem domain is shared informal meals within trusted communities - such as university campuses and workplaces - that extend beyond a person's immediate circle of close friends. Sharing a meal is one of the easiest ways to explore new local restaurants, unwind after classes or work, or celebrate holidays away from family. On campuses, people also occasionally gather at dining halls for quick weeknight meals or subsidized group dinners. While people sometimes choose to eat alone for quiet downtime, there are frequent occasions when someone wants company at a table but their close friends are busy or out of town.

When their immediate circle is unavailable, people turn to broader communities they belong to: dorms, classes, research labs, clubs, or workplace teams. To do this, they communicate through mailing lists, class forums, and existing group chats, as their shared membership gives acquaintances a baseline of trust that makes eating together feel comfortable. Three groups are involved in these meals:
- **Hosts** are community members - students, coworkers, undergraduate academic advisors, or Graduate Resident Advisors (GRAs, live-in dorm mentors who often host meals using dorm budgets or dining-hall swipes) - who want company for a meal or want to help peers connect. They usually have a time window (and often a restaurant or dining hall) in mind, and need firm commitments before booking a table.
- **Prospective guests** are peers in those same channels who want to join a meal to try a new restaurant or avoid eating alone, without having to rally a group from scratch or feel like they are intruding on an existing clique. Unlike close friends, acquaintances do not know each other's dietary restrictions, price sensitivity, or cuisine tastes.
- **Bystanders** are peers in those same channels who aren't interested, whether they are busy or would rather eat alone, yet end up dealing with noisy channels when meal planning devolves into long back-and-forth threads.

Currently, hosts who want to open a meal beyond their close friends send static messages in group chats or mailing lists, or share makeshift spreadsheets and forms. Social event planning tools (such as Partiful) and existing restaurant booking platforms (such as OpenTable) each have their niche: Partiful works well for collecting RSVPs once a host already knows what event they are throwing, while OpenTable handles booking a table once the restaurant and headcount are settled. However, neither helps an acquaintance group move smoothly from "who is free?" to an agreed-upon restaurant and a complete reservation.


## Bad situations

Across both ordinary weeks and holidays, existing channels fail informal meal groups at three recurring points: shy peers stall trying to align unfamiliar diets and budgets, peer hosts lose commitments ahead of booking deadlines without a clean way to reopen seats, and community mentors watch static signup sheets lock out interested residents.

### Bad Situation 1: Shy Holiday-Break Straggler
#### Steps Leading Up:
Noah is an international student from Singapore living in a campus dorm. Flying home for a four-day Thanksgiving break, or any other short holiday, is far too expensive, so he stays behind while his close friends travel home. However, Noah is still part of broader trusted communities, including his dorm mailing list, cultural club chat, and class forums.

#### Moment of Difficulty:
Although Noah's family does not traditionally celebrate Thanksgiving, it feels isolating to know that all his friends are home celebrating with their families while he is stuck on campus; Noah would love to share a Thanksgiving dinner rather than eat alone in the dining hall. Because Noah is shy, posting *"Anyone around for Thanksgiving dinner?"* feels exposing, and scrolling older messages leaves him unsure whether any existing plans still have room. When he finally posts, five acquaintances message him with *"maybe, depends where and how much."* Unlike his close friends, Noah does not know who is vegetarian or halal, what their budgets look like, or who refuses to walk far in the cold. Every restaurant he suggests hits polite pushback over price, diet, or distance, and nobody wants to be the demanding stranger who forces the choice.

#### Consequences:
By the time the back-and-forth drags late into the night, people bail to grab fast food or drift off, leaving Noah eating alone anyway. Additionally, peers who saw his post a few hours late assumed the group had already formed and ate alone rather than risk intruding, a stall that repeats across holidays, long weekends, and summer internships whenever close circles scatter.


### Bad Situation 2: Weeknight Restaurant Explorer Trapped by Soft Replies and Stale Invites
#### Steps Leading Up:
Alice recently started working at a new job and wants to try a neighborhood tapas spot on Thursday night. Her close friends are swamped or live across town, and the restaurant requires a firm headcount by Wednesday evening to hold a table of four to six. She wants to open extra seats to coworkers in a team Slack so she can share dishes, split the bill, and get to know acquaintances she only nods to in passing.

#### Moment of Difficulty:
On Tuesday, Alice drops a casual note: *"Thinking of trying dinner at 7:30 Thursday, anyone want to join?"* Rather than clear commitments she can book against, she fields scattered questions - *"How pricey?"*, *"Still room?"*, *"Can I bring a friend?"* - and ends up repeating the same answers across side messages while cluttering the channel for busy bystanders. By Tuesday night, four peers have replied *"sounds good!"* or *"maybe"*, so Alice assumes the table is full and tells a fifth interested coworker there are no more spots. On Wednesday afternoon, one of the four realizes they have a conflict and backs out over text. Because the original invite is buried in Slack history, Alice cannot gracefully let the channel know that seats have reopened without sending another noisy blast.

#### Consequences:
By Wednesday evening's booking deadline, Alice is stuck either locking in a smaller table - leaving out the fifth coworker she already turned away and other quiet peers who never knew seats reopened - or scrambling through DMs to find replacements. After repeating this herding effort a few times on ordinary weeknights, hosts stop opening tables to wider circles at all.


### Bad Situation 3: GRA and Advisor Meal-Swipe Spreadsheets Going Stale
#### Steps Leading Up:
Maya is a Graduate Resident Advisor (GRA) for a student dorm floor. She receives a recurring budget and guest meal swipes meant to help students step away from coursework and bond over informal meals. Every weekend, Maya wants to take ten residents to a campus dining hall or nearby casual spot without turning it into a stiff, formal event.

#### Moment of Difficulty:
To cap group size, Maya emails the floor a shared Google Sheet with ten signup rows. Within hours, the sheet breaks down in two ways. First, when slots fill quickly, the sheet looks finalized to anyone who checks email later; even if a student later removes their name because of a midterm conflict, past viewers have no reason to reopen the spreadsheet and never learn a spot opened unless Maya emails the whole floor again. Second, when slots fill slowly, residents cannot tell whether the meal is still happening, so hesitant residents skip signing up.

#### Consequences:
Maya arrives at the dining hall with empty spots that could have gone to interested residents on the same floor who saw a full sheet earlier and ate alone instead. Over the semester, GRAs and advisors tire of chasing spreadsheets and answering one-off emails, and cut back on hosting informal weekend meals, shrinking one of the easiest ways for busy students to meet peers outside their classes.


## Corroboration
The local evidence that bears on the seat, rather than on loneliness in general, is Silk's account of the Maseeh-night sheet: a fixed number of slots, filled by names, closed to anyone who saw the email late. A classmate who has spent Thanksgiving on campus also recognized the situation in our pitch. Both are single accounts. They make the situation familiar at MIT. They do not show how often it happens.

Yale and Buffalo are the public version of the host's count. Thanksgiving at Home tells students that a place depends on a host's confirmation, and it asks them to keep the day free because a late cancellation falls on a family that has already planned the meal. Thanksgiving without Borders says registration does not guarantee placement, and that a last-minute cancellation wastes the host's time and money. These offices already treat a casual yes as too weak to cook from, and they still leave some students without a place. They also cover one holiday, not a GRA dinner in March or a game night on a long weekend. They do not show that software would create more hosts. They show that a list of interested people is not yet a seat.

Forum posts are weaker, and they point in two directions. In a Berkeley thread, an international student staying in the dorms while friends went home wrote about being lonely, with dining limited on top of that. One reply, from someone who had stayed, described an almost empty building and said the quiet had been a relief. In a Maryland thread, an international student who could not leave asked what to do once dining halls closed, and a reply pointed toward faculty who host students. That reply only helps if the student already knows the list exists, which is Noah's problem in another form. These are anonymous posts from other campuses, and people who had a hard week are more likely to write them. They do support a distinction we should keep: some students want company over a break, and some want the quiet. The gatherings in this project are for the first group. Students in the second group should not have to do anything.

The studies explain why Noah hesitates to post, and they do not explain the spreadsheet. Epley and Schroeder found that commuters told to talk with a nearby stranger reported a better ride than commuters told to sit alone, while a separate group predicted the opposite, in part because they underestimated the stranger's willingness (Mistakenly Seeking Solitude, 2014). A train is not a potluck. The relevant piece is the hesitation before the first message. Chandler and colleagues surveyed 255 students at one UK university and found higher loneliness among those who ate alone because they were uneasy reaching out, uncomfortable eating with others, or had nobody to eat with. About 16% scored in the severe range on the ULS-8. The study is cross-sectional, so it cannot say that eating alone caused the loneliness, and the campus is not ours (Chandler et al., 2026). Interviews with 13 students at an English university, about meals before the 2020 lockdown, split the same way as the Berkeley thread. Some students wanted the solo meal as a break. Others avoided the canteen so they would not be seen sitting alone (Food and Foodways, 2024). An invitation can matter to the second group. It should not be imposed on the first.


## Workarounds and comparables
- invites:
  - Group chats
  - Forums like piazza or commons
  - Lab slacks
  - partiful
  - Etc.
- Managing resources/headcounts:
  - Google sheets
  - Google forms


Group chats are what Noah already has. They reach the people in the chat, and over a break many of those people have left. A message asking for company can go unanswered even when someone nearby would have come. A reply of "I might come" also does not hold a place, and the silence is visible to the rest of the chat, which is part of why the next ask does not get sent.

A spreadsheet gives the host a cap, a list of names, and one link. Maseeh nights ran on that. Students still have to see the email before the cells fill, and a student who changes plans has to remember to delete a name. If they do not, the slot stays occupied. If a home address is in the same email, forwarding the invitation forwards the address. The sheet is good at collecting a list from people the host already knows how to email. It is poor at returning a freed slot to someone who was never on that email.

Yale's and Buffalo's offices add staff, a warning that the meal is a real commitment, and a match between students and hosts. They are the serious version of holiday hospitality. As noted above, they still cannot place every student who signs up, and they do not cover a GRA dinner or a lounge game night outside that one holiday.

Walking over to a table or a lounge works when it is obvious that someone wants another person there. The English interviews suggest that some students skip shared dining rooms because they do not want to be seen alone. For them, the approach itself is the hard part. Knowing in advance that a particular gathering has a place for them is a different action from crossing a room.

Partiful and Facebook Events collect invitations and RSVPs, which is the right job when a host has already decided whom to invite and is willing to run an event page. They are a heavy tool for "a few of us are in the lounge, and there is a fourth chair." Services that assign dinner companions among strangers start from the opposite end: the other people are the product, and the host is not someone who was already going to be there. Our case starts with that host, and with a community the guest already belongs to.

None of these returns a released place to other people in the community who never got the original message, and none of them lets news of a home gathering circulate while the street address stays with the people who have claimed a place. Several critiques suggested profiles so a guest could judge whether they would get along with a host. A profile might answer that question. It would not tell Noah that a Maseeh slot had opened, or that he was allowed to take it.

## Solution sketch

Our solution is a **link-first shared meal organizer** built for trusted communities such as university campuses and workplaces. Rather than forcing people to abandon the mailing lists, Slacks, and group chats they already check, a host creates a lightweight **table page** and drops its shareable link directly into those existing channels (much like sharing a Zoom link).

1. **Public details without DM clutter (addresses BS1, BS2, and BS3):** Anyone who opens the link immediately sees the meal's time window, seat capacity, how many spots remain, budget tier, dietary notes (such as vegetarian or halal options), and the RSVP cutoff - whether it is a neighborhood tapas spot, a restaurant shortlist, or a GRA dining-hall trip. Shy peers like Noah never have to send an awkward *"is there still room?"* message, and hosts like Alice and Maya do not have to repeat the same details across private messages or emails.

2. **Two modes for choosing a restaurant, including progressive commitment (addresses BS1 and BS2):**
   - **Fixed venue:** When a host already has a restaurant or dining hall in mind (as in Alice's tapas outing or Maya's floor dinner), guests simply claim an open seat.
   - **Progressive discovery:** When an organizer has a time window but wants the group to choose together without hitting acquaintance deadlock (as in Noah's break dinner), the host sets a time, seat cap, and optional initial restaurant suggestions. Users can save a lightweight **dining preference profile** (dietary restrictions, price range, cuisine tastes, and distance) that helps suggest compatible local restaurants and quietly surfaces group constraints without putting anyone on the spot. Once enough diners commit (or ahead of the cutoff), committed guests vote from the compatible shortlist to lock in one restaurant. Importantly, we avoid personality or social compatibility matching; preferences exist purely to narrow down where everyone can eat.

3. **Verified community access without manual approval (addresses BS1 and BS2):** The host selects which community can join (for example, anyone with an `@mit.edu` or company email, or a specific dorm/group). Even if the link is forwarded, claiming a seat requires verifying membership in that community. Within those bounds, eligible peers claim an open spot directly without waiting for host approval, avoiding both host bottlenecking and the social discomfort of asking to be accepted.

4. **Live seat tracking, cutoffs, and automatic reopening (addresses BS2 and BS3):** Because every guest holds an explicit seat rather than a soft *"maybe"*, the host gets an exact headcount by the booking deadline. Holding a seat triggers a timely reminder ahead of the cutoff; if a diner's plans change and they release their spot before then, the seat automatically becomes open again on the exact same link and notifies anyone on a waitlist. Once the headcount and restaurant lock in at the cutoff, the host places the actual reservation (for example, on OpenTable or by phone) and marks the table booked.

5. **Community table board alongside shareable links (addresses BS1):** Inside each verified community, an optional day-by-day list displays open tables with remaining seats - answering the basic question *"what meals have room tonight?"* without requiring a hesitant newcomer to post into a silent hundred-person channel first.

**Scope and realistic limits:** The system cannot force someone to show up after a booking deadline passes, nor can it guarantee that a meal turns into a close friendship. It does, however, remove the friction between *"anyone free?"* and a confirmed reservation: dead spreadsheet rows and dropped seats reopen automatically, acquaintances align on compatible restaurants without awkward negotiation, and people who prefer eating alone never have to interact with the tool. We plan to pilot first within MIT communities (across both ordinary weeks and academic breaks) before expanding to other campuses and workplaces.

## Ethical analysis
The analysis below uses the four lecture dimensions required by F2. Each entry identifies the dimension, the relevant Envisioning Cards prompt, and the design choice it informs. Multi-lifespan cards are outside the scope of this assignment.

**Indirect stakeholders:** home addresses. A potluck at a professor's apartment also commits roommates and neighbors, who cannot opt out and have no account. A street address on the shareable card follows every forward. The card shows the general area, and the address is visible only while someone holds a place. Dining halls and lounges can be listed in full. A guest can still pass an address on later.

**Values:** community and dignity in who gets a place. Approval of each guest protects an evening, and it makes a spare seat depend on whether a stranger seems pleasant. Profiles sort people before they meet. The host sets community, capacity, and terms such as food or a contribution. Anyone inside those terms claims an open place. Nobody is ranked. Hosts can no longer turn one person away. Guests and reluctant hosts both avoid that rejection.

**Time:** non-use and the invitation link. If a gathering exists only in the app, students who never install it miss an open place the way they missed the Maseeh email. Each card has a link the host can send in the old chat or mailing list. Claiming still checks membership in the selected community. Reminders go only to people holding a place. Students who want a quiet break are not prompted to attend.

**Pervasiveness:** what "bring something" comes to mean. If most cards tell guests to bring food, students who stayed because travel was already expensive are shut out, and an optional dish starts to sound required. The default is that nothing is required. A host marks a contribution optional or expected. A dietary or religious note on the same card helps a guest who cannot eat the meal, and a host who would otherwise guess.

**Values:** community and safety at private homes. An open board would show Noah more gatherings, and it would let someone outside the community claim a place at an apartment. Some first-years are under 18. Claims stay in the community the host selects, and a home address stays off the shareable card. Shared affiliation is not safety. Home stays allowed, because hosts already use email, and there the address circulates with no check.

**Time:** reappropriation, once a place means something else. If open places are used for dates or large parties, a student looking for a small meal can no longer trust the day list. Types stay meals, potlucks, and game nights, and a card closes with its window. There is no follower list. A released place returns as a seat. Some misuse will remain, and the list can still mean a small gathering has room.
